Introduction
Brain tumors represent one of the most aggressive and clinically challenging 
neurological disorders, with their early and precise diagnosis being paramount for 
determining appropriate treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes. As the 
primary diagnostic modality for brain tumor detection, Magnetic Resonance Imaging 
(MRI) offers exceptional soft tissue contrast and non-invasive visualization 
capabilities that are indispensable in clinical neurology. However, the manual 
interpretation of MRI scans remains fraught with significant challenges that 
compromise diagnostic reliability, including substantial inter-observer variability 
among radiologists, the inherent subjectivity of visual assessment, and the growing 
problem of diagnostic fatigue in high-volume clinical settings. These limitations 
become particularly pronounced when attempting to differentiate between common 
tumor types such as gliomas (the most aggressive primary brain tumors), 
meningiomas (typically benign but potentially problematic tumors), and pituitary 
tumors (which present unique diagnostic challenges due to their anatomical location). 
Traditionally, brain tumors have been detected using imaging techniques such as 
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computer tomography (CT) scans. These 
methods rely on the visual assessment of scans by trained radiologists to identify 
abnormalities. However, Conventional techniques for detecting brain tumors have 
limitations such as variability in human interpretation and the difficulty of detecting 
subtle early signs of tumors. Due to these challenges machine learning (ML), 
especially transfer learning (TL) has been employed in the early detection and 
classification of brain tumors. 
Computer-Aided Diagnosis (CAD) systems leveraging deep learning and transfer 
learning paradigms have been deployed. Although standard Convolutional Neural 
Networks (CNNs) demonstrate strong capabilities in local spatial feature extraction, 
traditional architectures encounter operational limitations when applied to neuro-
oncologic imaging:
1. Sensitivity to Irrelevant Spatial Artifacts: Standard CNN backbones process 
background cranial bone, soft tissue, and acquisition noise uniformly alongside 
parenchymal pathology, degrading feature representation purity.
2. Receptive Field and Contextual Bottlenecks: Standard localized convolutional

kernels struggle to capture broad multi-scale spatial dependencies and context 
across complex, heterogeneous tumor microenvironments.
3. Loss of Spatial Coordinate Geometry in Feature Fusion: Naive feature fusion 
methods collapse 2D spatial feature maps via premature Global Average Pooling 
(GAP) prior to vector concatenation or element-wise addition, destroying 2D 
coordinate layouts and restricting explainability.
4. Predictive Opacity and Lack of Reliability Quantification: Conventional point-
estimate deep classifiers do not quantify diagnostic uncertainty, offering 
clinicians no indication of when a model prediction is ambiguous or 
unconfident.
Literature Survey
Baseline Model: An attention-fused architecture for brain tumor diagnosis 
Arash Hekmat, Zuping Zhang, Saif Ur Rehman Khan, Ifza Shad, Omair Bilal, An attention-fused 
architecture for brain tumor diagnosis, Biomedical Signal Processing and Control, Volume 101, 
2025, 107221, ISSN 1746-8094, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bspc.2024.107221.
The paper introduces a novel method, the Attention-Fused MobileNet-LSTM model, 
which aims to improve the classification accuracy on Brain Tumor MRIs. By 
integrating features from two well recognized pre-trained convolutional neural 
network (CNN) models named as MobileNetV1 and MobileNetV2, the paper has 
developed a reliable model. Their approach addresses challenges in CNN training, 
including high processing demands, difficulty in discerning intricate details in 
images, and diminished efficacy due to noise or irrelevant data. They employ a 
transformer aid in feature extraction, while gamma correction before features fusion 
tackles long-range relationship capture between successive features. The proposed 
model employs Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent neural networks (RNN) 
for direct class label prediction and understanding the intermediate features from 
feature fusion. 
They leverage a publicly available brain tumor dataset comprising 3,000 MRI 
images, on which they conduct augmentation procedures and standardization to meet 
the CNN’s image resizing prerequisites, thereby enhancing dataset diversity and 
classification task performance. With a consistent accuracy of 98.66%, their proposed 
model showcases significant potential as a reliable tool for precise classification of 
normal and malignant brain MRIs in the medical domain. Additionally, they employ 
Grad-CAM to visualize the key regions prioritized by their proposed model, offering 
insights into their decision-making processes. 
Operational Workflow
1. Transformer Attention Preprocessing: Raw MRI frames are passed through a 
Vision Transformer (ViTL16) self-attention encoder to suppress non-brain 
anatomical structures and background noise.

2. Photometric Luminance (Gamma) Calibration: The attention-filtered 
representation undergoes gamma correction to calibrate contrast across dynamic 
ranges.
3. Dual Backbone Extraction: The contrast-calibrated image is fed simultaneously 
into two parallel pre-trained backbones: MobileNetV1 (86 layers, 3.23 million 
parameters) and MobileNetV2 (154 layers, 2.26 million parameters).
4. Vector Flattening and Concatenation: Output feature maps are flattened via 
Global Average Pooling (GAP) and merged in a 1D vector concatenation layer, 
yielding a 2,304-dimensional vector.
5. Recurrent Sequence Modeling: The concatenated vector is routed into a Long 
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent network to capture inter-feature 
sequential dependencies prior to SoftMax probability projection.

their feature representations overlap significantly. Fusing two similar models 
provides lower information gain compared to combining complementary, 
functionally specialized backbones.
●
Premature Spatial Collapse: Applying Global Average Pooling (GAP) before 
feature fusion flattens 7 x 7 2D spatial feature maps into 1D vectors. This 
discards 2D spatial coordinates prior to cross-feature interaction, preventing the 
model from performing explicit spatial query-key alignment across branches.
●
Absence of Predictive Uncertainty Quantification: The AFML network outputs 
single point-estimate class probabilities. It cannot measure predictive variance 
or clinical uncertainty, offering no mechanism to flag ambiguous scans for 
expert radiologist review.
Methodology
Proposed Model: EfficientNet-DenseNet Network with Bidirectional Cross-
Attention and parallel GradCAM mechanism (ED-XAGN)
Rationale and Architectural Updates
1. Backbone Replacement for Functional Specialization: We replace MobileNetV1 
and MobileNetV2 with two complementary, structurally distinct CNN 
backbones:
○
EfficientNet-B0 (Local Spatial & Texture): Utilizes compound scaling 
(balancing depth, width, and resolution) with mobile inverted bottleneck 
(MBConv) blocks and built-in Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) channel 
attention. It extracts local boundary geometry, fine tumor edge transitions, 
micro-calcifications, and surface textures.
○
DenseNet-121 (Multi-Scale Semantic): Utilizes direct shortcut connections 
where every layer receives concatenated feature maps from all preceding 
layers. It preserves low-level, mid-level, and high-level feature maps 
simultaneously, capturing rich multi-scale semantic abstractions and long-
range anatomical context.
2. Removal of LSTM: We eliminate the LSTM module entirely. Instead of treating 
static spatial vectors as dynamic time series, feature interaction is handled by an 
explicit, transparent Bidirectional Cross-Attention Engine that operates directly 
on 2D spatial feature maps.
3. Preservation of 2D Spatial Layout: Feature maps are tapped prior to Global 
Average Pooling at their spatial resolution (7×7), maintaining 2D spatial 
coordinates throughout cross-attention querying and gated fusion.
4. Single-Stream Early Parallel Grad-CAM Mechanism: We eliminate the late-
stage Grad-CAM overhead and replace it entirely with a single-stream early 
parallel Grad-CAM head attached directly to an intermediate stage of 
EfficientNet-B0 (14×14 resolution). This generates a high-resolution early

5. Clinical Decision Support and Predictive Uncertainty: The output stage 
incorporates a dual-head multi-layer perceptron (MLP) containing a primary 
classification head alongside an Uncertainty Estimation Head. Scans with high 
predictive variance are flagged automatically for escalation.

Conclusion
The upgraded EfficientNet-DenseNet Network with Bidirectional Cross-Attention 
and parallel GradCAM mechanism (ED-XAGN) directly addresses the architectural 
limitations of the older AFML base model. By replacing MobileNetV1/V2 with 
EfficientNet-B0 (local spatial specialist) and DenseNet-121 (multi-scale semantic 
specialist), the network maximizes feature diversity. Removing the LSTM module in 
favor of symmetric bidirectional cross-attention and ASCA gated fusion preserves 
2D spatial layouts, enhances classification accuracy, and produces clear, artifact-free 
Grad-CAM visual heatmaps. Finally, the integrated predictive uncertainty head 
provides clinicians with a confidence metric, establishing a reliable framework for 
brain tumor diagnosis.

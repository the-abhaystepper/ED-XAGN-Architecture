A B S T R A C T
To enhance the accuracy of brain tumor diagnosis and treatment, reliance on MRI images is crucial.
However, human error in manual diagnosis remains a concern, underscoring the urgency for robust
TL (Transfer Learning) and CNN (Convolutional Neural Network) based techniques to enhance
accuracy in discerning normal and malignant brain tumors. Our paper introduces a novel method, the
EfficientNet-DenseNet Network with Bidirectional Cross-Attention and early GradCAM mechanism
(ED-XAGN) model, aimed at improving classification accuracy. By integrating features from two
complementary pre-trained CNN models: EfficientNetB0 and DenseNet-121, we have developed an
architecture superior to comparable ones on the market. Our approach addresses challenges commonly
found in tumor detection systems, including high processing demands, difficulty in discerning intricate
details in images, and diminished efficacy due to noise or irrelevant data. Our architecture employs
Gamma Correction techniques to reduce degradation in long-form feature capture, the twin-CNN
backbone is augmented with a Bidirectional Cross-Attention module which allows both feature
streams to query each other, it also introduces a novel fusion architecture called Adaptive Spatial-
Channel Attention (ASCA) to optimize the weightages of local and far-form spatial features. We make
use of a publicly available brain tumor dataset consisting of 7,200 MRI images, on which we apply
various augmentations and standardization techniques to represent real-world conditions and enhance
model compatibility.
1. Introduction
A tumor is an abnormal growth of cells that can be
benign or malignant. When this growth occurs in the brain,
it is known as a brain tumor (1). Brain tumors represent one
of the most aggressive and clinically challenging neurolog-
ical disorders, with their early and precise diagnosis being
paramount for determining appropriate treatment strategies
and improving patient outcomes. As the primary diagnostic
modality for brain tumor detection, Magnetic Resonance
Imaging (MRI) (2) offers exceptional soft tissue contrast and
non-invasive visualization capabilities that are indispensable
in clinical neurology. However, the manual interpretation
of MRI scans remains fraught with significant challenges
that compromise diagnostic reliability, including substantial
inter-observer variability among radiologists, the inherent
subjectivity of visual assessment, and the growing problem
of diagnostic fatigue in high-volume clinical settings. These
limitations become particularly pronounced when attempt-
ing to differentiate between common tumor types such as
(3) (the most aggressive primary brain tumors), menin-
giomas (typically benign but potentially problematic tu-
mors), and pituitary tumors (which present unique diagnos-
tic challenges due to their anatomical location). The grow-
ing complexity of neuro-oncological diagnosis has spurred
remarkable advances in artificial intelligence, particularly
through the application of deep learning (4) for automated

2. Related Work
Automated computer-aided diagnosis systems for brain
tumor classification from MRI scans have evolved across
several deep learning paradigms. Convolutional Neural Net-
works utilizing transfer learning (5) (6) remain foundational
in neuro-imaging due to their ability to extract hierarchi-
cal spatial primitives from pre-trained natural image do-
mains. Early studies evaluated standard architectures in-
cluding AlexNet, VGG-16, and fine-tuned ResNet variants
(5), demonstrating automated tumor detection on benchmark
datasets like BraTS, but observing performance drops along
complex infiltrative tumor margins. Standalone models such
as DenseNet-121, DenseNet-201, InceptionV3, and Xcep-
tion (10) have been benchmarked extensively on datasets
like BR35H. However, individual CNN backbones often
struggle to balance fine-grained local boundary extraction
with broad multi-scale semantic context, driving researchers
toward multi-stream feature fusion frameworks.
To enhance feature selectivity and suppress non-brain
cranial noise, recent architectures incorporate attention mech-
anisms and Vision Transformers (ViTs) (7) (8) . Attention-
Guided CNNs (AG-CNN) highlight pathological regions
while suppressing healthy tissue background. Hybrid Trans-
former Enhanced CNNs (TECNN) (11) utilize self-attention
blocks from Vision Transformers to model long-range spa-
tial dependencies across axial slices. Similarly, ConvAt-
tenMixer (9) combines convolutional mixer blocks with
external self-attention mechanisms to differentiate glioma
subtypes. While transformer based attention enhances spa-
tial focus, applying pure Vision Transformers directly to
raw pixel grids imposes heavy parameter burdens and high
memory consumption.
Combining complementary representations from mul-
tiple deep backbones via feature fusion has emerged as
an effective strategy to boost classification performance.
Ensembles combining lightweight CNNs with post-fusion
attention guidance and sparse autoencoder networks like
TumorAwareNet (7) have demonstrated improvements over
single-stream baselines.
2.1. Baseline Study
Most recently, Hekmat et al. (12) proposed the Attention-
Fused MobileNet-LSTM (AFML) framework, combining
a ViT-L/16 attention pre-filter with gamma contrast cor-
rection, parallel MobileNetV1 and MobileNetV2 feature
extraction, post-GAP vector concatenation, and an LSTM
recurrent unit for classification.
2.1.1. Architecture
1. Transformer Attention Preprocessing: Raw MRI frames
are passed through a Vision Transformer (ViTL16)
self-attention encoder to suppress non-brain anatomi-
cal structures and background noise.
2. Photometric Luminance (Gamma) Calibration: The
attention-filtered representation undergoes gamma
correction to calibrate contrast across dynamic ranges.
3. Dual Backbone Extraction: The contrast-calibrated
image is fed simultaneously into two parallel pre-
trained backbones: MobileNetV1 (16) (86 layers, 3.23
million parameters) and MobileNetV2 (17) (154 lay-
ers, 2.26 million parameters).
4. Vector Flattening and Concatenation: Output feature
maps are flattened via Global Average Pooling (GAP)
and merged in a 1D vector concatenation layer, yield-
ing a 2,304-dimensional vector.
5. Recurrent Sequence Modeling: The concatenated vec-
tor is routed into a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
(18) recurrent network to capture inter-feature se-
quential dependencies prior to SoftMax probability
projection.
Figure 1: AFML Architecture
Although AFML achieved strong accuracy on binary
benchmarks, its performance relied on concatenating struc-
turally redundant lightweight encoders and unrolling static
spatial vectors through an opaque LSTM module. Further-
more, on severely class-imbalanced datasets (such as the
INbreast benchmark), AFML exhibited a major failure mode
where malignant recall dropped to 0.12 (12%). Some other
limitations discovered in the architecture are as follows:
1. Architectural Redundancy and also Low Information
Gain: Both MobileNetV1 and MobileNetV2 belong
to the same lightweight architectural family, relying
heavily on depthwise separable convolutions and in-
verted residual bottleneck blocks. Because their fea-
ture extraction mechanisms are structurally similar,
their feature representations overlap significantly. Fus-
ing two similar models provides lower information
gain compared to combining complementary, func-
tionally specialized backbones.
2. Premature Spatial Collapse: Applying Global Aver-
age Pooling (GAP) before feature fusion flattens 7
x 7 2D spatial feature maps into 1D vectors. This
discards 2D spatial coordinates prior to cross-feature
interaction, preventing the model from performing
explicit spatial query-key alignment across branches.
3. Absence of Predictive Uncertainty Quantification:
The AFML network outputs single point-estimate

3. Methodology
The proposed architecture, although sharing the same
base as the AFML model, incorporates several key improve-
ments which enables it to address the limitations of the
baseline whilst being ultra-compact and keeping compute to
a minimum. The modifications (in brief) are as follows:
1. Backbone Replacement for Functional Specialization:
We replace MobileNetV1 and MobileNetV2 with two
complementary, structurally distinct CNN backbones:
(a) EfficientNet-B0 (Local Spatial and Texture)

(b) DenseNet-121 (Multi-Scale Semantic) (14): Uti-

2. LSTM Removal: We eliminate the LSTM module en-
tirely. Instead of treating static spatial vectors as dy-
namic time series, feature interaction is handled by
an explicit, transparent Bidirectional Cross-Attention
Engine that operates directly on 2D spatial feature
maps.
3. Preservation of 2D Spatial Layout: Feature maps are
tapped prior to Global Average Pooling at their spatial
resolution (7×7), maintaining 2D spatial coordinates
throughout cross-attention querying and gated fusion.
4. Single-Stream Early Parallel Grad-CAM Mechanism:
We eliminate the late-stage Grad-CAM overhead and
replace it entirely with a single-stream early parallel
Grad-CAM head attached directly to an intermedi-
ate stage of EfficientNet-B0 (14×14 resolution). This
generates a high-resolution early Grad-CAM heatmap
focused on local structural edge boundaries in parallel
with the main cross-attention pipeline.
5. Clinical Decision Support and Predictive Uncer-
tainty: The output stage incorporates a dual-head
multi-layer perceptron (MLP) containing a primary
classification head alongside an Uncertainty Estima-
tion Head. Scans with high predictive variance are
flagged automatically for escalation.

3.2. Dataset Preprocessing and Augmentation
The dataset included images which were either too noisy
for use in the model training or whose sizes varied too widely
to be compatible with the CNN backbone of our architecture.
To address these issues, as well as to diversify the dataset,
we applied certain preprocessing and augmentation methods
which are listed below:
1. Rescale: Each image in our dataset was rescaled by
dividing the pixel values by 255. This normaliza-
tion ensures that the images are within a standard
dimension range of [0,1], preparing them for effective
processing by the CNN.
2. Rotations: We introduced a 90-degree rotation for
each image in the dataset. This augmentation tech-
nique generates additional variations of the original
images. By doing so, the model becomes more adept
at recognizing objects from various orientations, en-
hancing their robustness.
3. Vertical and Horizontal Flipping: Both vertical and
horizontal flips were applied to the images. A horizon-
tal flip creates mirror- image versions of each original
image, which aids the model in recognizing objects
from both left-to-right and right-to-left perspectives.
Similarly, a vertical flip produces top-to-bottom mir-
rored versions of each image. This augmentation helps
the model to learn object recognition from vertical
reversals as well.
Using the above image augmentation techniques like
rescaling, rotating, and flipping helps to increase the di-
versity of the training data, which is crucial for improving
the robustness of a ML model. These techniques prevent
overfitting by ensuring the model is trained on varied repre-
sentations of the same images, thereby enhancing its ability
to generalize to new, unseen data.
3.3. Proposed Architecture
At its core, the framework first standardizes contrast
shifts across raw MRI scans using dynamic gamma calibra-
tion to mitigate spatial feature degradation and interpretabil-
ity bottlenecks. The normalized input is simultaneously
routed into two parallel, functionally specialized feature
extraction streams- an EfficientNet-B0 backbone dedicated
to isolating high-frequency, local spatial boundaries and
related micro-textures, and a DenseNet-121 backbone de-
signed to capture multi-scale semantic abstractions amongst
other broad anatomical context.
To preserve spatial geometry and avoid premature in-
formation collapse, feature maps are tapped prior to Global
Average Pooling (GAP)and projected into a shared latent
space. Within this space, a symmetric bidirectional cross-
attention module enables the streams to mutually query each
other, aligning localized edge details with global context.
These cross-attended feature maps are then integrated via

5. Conclusion and Future Work
5.1. Conclusion
In this work, we presented ED-XAGN, a novel deep
learning framework designed to overcome the spatial infor-
mation collapse, feature redundancy, and opacity inherent in
conventional computer-aided brain tumor diagnosis systems.
By replacing uniform backbones with two functionally spe-
cialized feature extractors—EfficientNet-B0 for fine local
boundaries and micro-textures, and DenseNet-121 for multi-
scale semantic context—the model captures comprehensive
pathological representations. Preserving 2D spatial coordi-
nate geometry prior to global pooling allows the symmetric
bidirectional cross-attention engine to mutually align local-
ized spatial queries with broad anatomical structures, while
the Adaptive Spatial-Channel Gated Fusion (ASCA) en-
gine dynamically recalibrates spatial and channel relevance
on a scan-by-scan basis. Crucially, ED-XAGN bridges the
gap between predictive performance and clinical usability
by coupling an early parallel Grad-CAM head for high-
resolution edge attributions with a Monte Carlo dropout-
based uncertainty head that quantifies decision confidence
and automatically escalates ambiguous scans for expert ra-
diologist review. Together, these innovations establish ED-
XAGN as an accurate, interpretable, and clinically self-
aware solution for automated neuro-oncological assessment.
5.2. Future Work
While ED-XAGN demonstrates strong predictive perfor-
mance and clinical interpretability, several routes exist for
future investigation:
1. Cross-Institutional Validation: Evaluating the archi-
tecture across multi-center, cross-institutional MRI
datasets will validate its domain generalization capa-
bilities against varying hospital scanner protocols and
artifact distributions.
2. 3D Volumetric and Multi-Sequence Extension: Ex-
tending the 2D slice-level cross-attention mecha-
nism to 3D volumetric MRI representations, which
would involve incorporating multi-sequence modali-
ties such as T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR
scans—could provide richer 3D spatial context.
3. Active Learning and Human-in-the-Loop Integration:

into an active learning framework will allow high-
variance predictions to be automatically flagged for
